# Scene Event Engine

## 1. Purpose

The story should feel like two specific people **experiencing things together**, not like a sequence of romantic poses placed in different backgrounds.

A useful creative unit is the **Scene Event**:

> a specific situation in a specific place that gives at least one character something to do, notice, decide, react to, recover from, or carry into the next moment.

Scene Events are a thinking tool, not a rigid screenplay formula.

## 2. Constraint levels

Use three levels of guidance.

### REQUIRED — quality floor

These are the few constraints that should normally be protected:

- Major scenes must contain a concrete, observable situation rather than only an emotional label.
- Avoid vague placeholders such as “互动一下 / 甜蜜游玩 / 看风景 / 开心地走”.
- Character behavior must be visible through action or reaction when the scene is relationship-relevant.
- Important activities must respect basic physical reality.
- For complete Vlogs, the scene route must make the actual day / experience understandable before detailed shots are written.

These rules protect clarity, not formula.

### PREFERRED — strong quality enhancers

Use when they improve the story:

- trigger → action → reaction → aftermath,
- before → during → after for high-value activities,
- scene-to-scene state carryover,
- asymmetric behavior between the two characters,
- delayed reactions and small reversals,
- props that continue across scenes,
- a later callback to something established earlier,
- a scene whose ending naturally creates the next scene.

Do not force all of them into every scene.

### OPTIONAL — creative expansion

Use freely when appropriate:

- recurring visual motif,
- running joke,
- role reversal,
- failed attempt,
- tiny misunderstanding,
- competition,
- surprise,
- interruption,
- accidental reveal of personality,
- environmental obstacle,
- object-based callback,
- expectation/payoff,
- parallel or contrast editing.

These are creative ingredients, not requirements.

## 3. Mini-story lens

Some scenes are only fragments. Others are strong enough to become a **mini-story** inside the larger video.

A mini-story is not a mandatory unit, does not need to appear at a fixed frequency, and should not be added simply because the skill mentions it.

Use it as a creative possibility: when a situation naturally contains enough change, reaction, escalation, reversal, recovery or emotional texture, consider giving that moment more room.

Examples:
- a roller coaster scene may include anticipation, the ride itself, involuntary reactions, and recovery afterward,
- a game may include choosing sides, playing, winning/losing, and teasing afterward,
- a meal may include ordering, discovering a preference, sharing/stealing food, and a small consequence,
- a navigation mistake may include confidence, getting lost, realizing it, and the partner's response.

One useful internal lens for such scenes is:

**Trigger → Action → Reaction → Counteraction / Aftermath → Exit State**

Other structures are equally valid. A mini-story may be built around anticipation, discovery, failed expectation, shared observation, escalation, reversal, repetition, silence, or another shape that better fits the material.

Example:

- Trigger: one character notices a strange accessory.
- Action: they put it on the partner without warning.
- Reaction: the partner removes it immediately and acts unimpressed.
- Aftermath: after the first character walks away, the partner quietly tries it again in the mirror.
- Exit state: the accessory becomes a prop that can return later.

This is stronger than:

> 她给他戴上发箍，他无奈地笑。

But do not expand a simple 2-second connective shot into a five-step mini-drama just to satisfy the model.

A mini-story may span multiple shots, scenes or generation segments. Story structure should be designed first; production segmentation comes later.

## 4. Scene state

A scene becomes more believable when something is different at the end.

Possible state changes:

- a character now carries a bag, drink, map, prize or souvenir,
- hair / clothing becomes slightly messy,
- confidence turns into embarrassment,
- one character becomes tired,
- the route decision changes,
- one character knows something the other does not,
- an inside joke is created,
- the couple switches who leads,
- daylight becomes evening,
- an object introduced earlier gains new meaning.

State change does not need to be dramatic.

A quiet scene can change only:
- pace,
- physical distance,
- energy,
- attention,
- or shared understanding.

## 5. State carryover

When useful, let the next scene inherit something from the previous one.

Examples:

- the shopping bag bought earlier is still in one character's hand during the rest scene,
- messy hair from the ride is being fixed while walking away,
- the map used at the entrance becomes crumpled later,
- the snack container continues into a walking scene,
- a silly accessory rejected earlier quietly returns in the ending.

This creates the feeling that time actually passed.

Do not force continuity props into every scene. Use them when they add story value or realism.

## 6. Event variety

Different backgrounds do not automatically create different scenes.

Watch for semantic repetition:

- walk → look → smile,
- walk → turn back → smile,
- walk → stand together → smile.

These may be three shots but only one behavior pattern.

Prefer variation in what the characters must **do**:

- choose,
- wait,
- navigate,
- compete,
- recover,
- eat,
- carry,
- search,
- miss each other,
- compare,
- help,
- hesitate,
- fail,
- decide,
- watch,
- rest,
- remember.

Repeated behavior can still be intentional when used as a motif, callback, escalation, intimacy pattern or deliberate stylistic choice.

Likewise, eye contact, hand-holding, hugging, kissing and other forms of intimacy are neither mandatory nor forbidden. Evaluate them by context and dramatic usefulness, not by category.

## 7. Event density versus breathing room

Rich content does not mean constant incident.

Use event density selectively:

- selected high-value scenes may grow into a multi-beat mini-story,
- ordinary scenes may contain one small believable action,
- quiet scenes may contain almost no action if their purpose is emotional deceleration,
- endings often benefit from less action after stronger earlier events.

The goal is **meaningful variation**, not maximum busyness.

## 8. Scene event test

Before locking a major scene, ask:

- What is physically happening here?
- What makes this scene different from the previous one?
- What behavior does this situation reveal?
- What, if anything, is different when the scene ends?
- Could this same scene be pasted into another location with almost no change?
- Is the scene present because it serves the experience, character, rhythm, continuity, or payoff?

If the answers are weak, redesign the event rather than only changing camera language.
