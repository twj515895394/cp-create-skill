# Scene Specificity and Content Map

## 1. The viewer must understand what the video actually contains

Before writing the director shot table, produce a **Content Map / Scene Route** for any complete Vlog.

The map must make the entire runtime understandable at a glance.

For a 60-second video, the reader should be able to answer:
- Where do the couple go?
- What concrete activities do they do?
- In what order?
- What relationship beat happens in each place?
- How does day/time/emotion change?
- What is the final payoff?

If the user cannot understand the video from this map, the plan is too vague.

## 2. Required content-map fields

Use a compact table with:

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Runtime range | Approximate section duration |
| Concrete place | Specific spatial setting, not just "outside" or "park" |
| Concrete activity | What they actually do |
| Relationship beat | What this reveals about the couple |
| Visual anchor | The distinctive physical thing visible in the scene |
| Transition | Why/how the story moves to the next place |

Example:
- Weak place: "游乐园"
- Better place: "过山车排队区与上车平台"
- Weak activity: "体验项目"
- Better activity: "排队、压下安全压杆、等待发车、俯冲时一人尖叫一人笑着侧看"

## 3. Ban generic activity language

Do not use generic placeholders when a concrete activity can be chosen.

Avoid:
- 游玩项目
- 逛一逛
- 互动一下
- 吃点东西
- 看风景
- 休息
- 玩设施

Prefer:
- 乘坐过山车
- 在射击摊位比拼分数
- 坐旋转木马
- 看花车巡游
- 在爆米花摊买焦糖爆米花
- 坐在喷泉边整理购物袋
- 在烟花观赏区提前占位置

## 4. Real-world activity realism

When designing an activity, include real physical stages and constraints.

### Roller coaster example
A believable roller-coaster scene may include:
- entering the queue,
- walking through railings,
- stepping into the car,
- sitting side by side,
- pulling down lap bar or shoulder restraint,
- staff check / waiting beat,
- train leaving slowly,
- lift hill / anticipation,
- drop / wind / hair movement,
- one partner screaming or gripping the restraint,
- the other reacting,
- post-ride laughter or unsteady walking.

Not every stage must appear, but the chosen beats must feel physically possible.

### Food stall example
A believable food beat may include:
- reading menu,
- pointing to an item,
- payment / receiving tray,
- opening packaging,
- first taste,
- reaction,
- offering a bite,
- wiping fingers,
- carrying the item while walking.

### Parade example
A believable parade beat may include:
- crowd forming,
- finding a sightline,
- turning toward music,
- reacting to passing performers,
- one partner pointing out a float,
- sharing a short comment,
- crowd noise continuing into transition.

## 5. Real venue rule

If the user names a real venue:
- do not invent branded attraction names unless known or provided,
- use concrete generic attraction types if exact venue facts are unnecessary,
- if exact venue layout or actual attractions materially matter, ask the user or use research tools when available.

Specificity must not become fabrication.

## 6. Spatial progression

Avoid teleporting between vague locations.

Show believable progression:
entrance → attraction area → food zone → rest area → evening promenade → fireworks viewing zone.

Transitions may use:
- walking,
- signage/map,
- transport,
- time-of-day change,
- carrying newly acquired props,
- sound bridge.

## 7. Visual anchors

Every major scene should have at least one concrete anchor:
- queue railings,
- coaster car and restraint,
- carousel horses,
- food tray,
- shopping bags,
- parade float,
- fountain bench,
- illuminated castle facade,
- fireworks launch area.

These anchors help both human crews and downstream generation models understand the scene.

## 8. Content density

A Vlog should not contain more locations than the runtime can support.

For short videos:
- fewer places,
- more specific interaction,
- more meaningful reactions.

Do not confuse “many places” with “rich content.”
